# Nutrients and fertilizer

## What fertilizer numbers mean
Fertilizer labels show N-P-K: nitrogen (leafy growth), phosphorus (roots and flowers) and potassium (fruit and overall health). Leafy greens and herbs like a balanced or nitrogen-rich feed; tomatoes, peppers and strawberries need more potassium once they flower, so switch to a tomato feed at that point.

## How often to feed container plants
Potting mix runs out of nutrients after about 4–6 weeks because watering washes them out. Feed container plants with a liquid fertilizer at half the label strength every 2–3 weeks during active growth. Do not feed seedlings until they have their first true leaves, and do not feed a plant that is wilting from dry soil: water first. Always follow the product label.

## Yellow leaves: nitrogen deficiency or overwatering?
Nitrogen deficiency makes the oldest, lowest leaves turn evenly pale yellow while new growth stays green but small, and the plant grows slowly. Overwatering also yellows lower leaves, but they feel soft and the soil stays wet. Check the soil moisture first: if the soil is wet, fix the watering; if the soil is fine and the plant has not been fed for more than a month, give a half-strength balanced feed.

## Too much fertilizer
Signs: brown, crispy leaf edges, a white crust on the soil surface, and lots of leaves but few flowers or fruit (too much nitrogen). Fix: flush the pot with plenty of plain water, skip feeding for a few weeks, and use a weaker mix afterwards.

## Organic options
Compost, worm castings and well-rotted manure improve soil and feed slowly. Mix a few handfuls of compost into the top of the soil at planting time. Liquid seaweed and fish emulsion are gentle organic feeds for containers.
